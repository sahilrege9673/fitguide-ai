The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommends that healthy adults engage in progressive resistance training at least 2 to 3 days per week to develop and maintain muscular strength, hypertrophy, and bone mineral density. 

### Core Resistance Training Principles
1. **Progressive Overload**: The gradual increase of stress placed upon the musculoskeletal system during training. This can be achieved systematically by increasing external load (weight), total training volume (sets multiplied by reps), execution tempo (lengthening eccentric control), or decreasing inter-set rest intervals. Without progressive overload, neuromuscular adaptations plateau.

2. **Repetition Ranges and Adaptation Specificity**:
   - **Strength Adaptation**: 1 to 6 repetitions per set at 80% to 100% of 1-Repetition Maximum (1RM), emphasizing heavy neural recruitment and motor unit synchronization. Inter-set rest periods should range from 2 to 5 minutes to restore intramuscular phosphagen (ATP-PC) stores.
   - **Hypertrophy (Muscle Growth)**: 6 to 12 repetitions per set at 65% to 80% of 1RM, targeting a balance of mechanical tension and metabolic accumulation. Inter-set rest periods should range from 60 to 120 seconds.
   - **Muscular Endurance**: 15 to 25 repetitions per set at lighter loads (<60% 1RM) with shorter rest intervals (30 to 60 seconds), enhancing capillary density and intracellular buffering capacity.

3. **Weekly Volume Thresholds**:
   For novice to intermediate trainees, 10 to 20 challenging working sets per muscle group per week distributed across 2 or 3 training sessions yields optimal muscle protein synthesis while mitigating systemic and localized overtraining. Working sets should be taken within 1 to 3 Reps in Reserve (RIR) or Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) 7 to 9. Training to complete muscular failure on every set is unnecessary and elevates injury risk and neuromuscular fatigue.

4. **Multi-Joint Compound Movements**:
   Compound multi-joint exercises—including squats, deadlifts, presses, rows, and pull-ups—should form the bedrock of resistance training programs. These movements recruit large muscle masses, elicit superior hormonal and metabolic responses, and promote functional kinetic chain integration. Isolation exercises (e.g., biceps curls, lateral raises, leg extensions) serve as complementary work to target specific muscular weak links.
